You're selling across African markets.

Customers are placing orders. Payments are coming in. Your sales numbers may even look healthy.

But the amount that ultimately reaches your business may tell a different story.

Processing fees, currency conversion, payouts, refunds, disputes and settlement delays can reduce the value of successful transactions. Failed payments and unavailable local payment methods can create another problem entirely: sales that never become completed transactions.

The result is a payment problem that is bigger than the gateway fee.

Your payment setup can affect how much you sell, how much you retain from each sale, how quickly you receive your money and how much work your team needs to manage it.

This guide shows you how to identify payment-conversion leakage, calculate the true cost of accepting payments, evaluate payment infrastructure across African markets and determine what should change before you expand further.

## Key Takeaways

- Payment costs are more than processing fees. FX, payouts, refunds, disputes, settlement and operational work can all affect the amount your business ultimately retains.
- Payment failures can reduce revenue before any payment cost is incurred. A customer who cannot complete checkout is a lost or delayed sales opportunity.
- African payment infrastructure needs to be evaluated market by market. Payment methods, currencies, settlement and merchant eligibility can differ between countries.
- The cheapest payment provider is not necessarily the most profitable. A route with a slightly higher fee can produce a stronger commercial result if it completes more transactions and reduces operational work.
- Settlement matters as much as acceptance. A successfully collected payment is not necessarily immediately usable cash.
- Provider evaluation should cover the entire payment operation, including collection, payout, settlement, reporting, reconciliation, integration, support, and expansion capability.
- A payment cost worksheet can help identify where the biggest commercial problem actually sits before you change providers.

## How Payment Problems Reduce E-commerce Margins in Africa

Payment infrastructure can reduce e-commerce margins in two ways: it can increase the cost of successful transactions and prevent potential transactions from being completed.

You may see the first problem when:

- processing fees reduce the value of each sale
- FX conversion reduces the amount received
- payout charges are deducted
- refunds or disputes create additional costs
- funds remain unavailable until settlement
- finance teams spend significant time reconciling payment activity

The second problem appears when:

- a customer's preferred payment method is unavailable
- a payment route performs poorly in a particular market
- a transaction remains pending
- a payment fails and the customer does not retry
- a customer abandons checkout after selecting a payment method

This means merchants need to answer two separate questions:

1. How much do we retain from a successful payment?

2. How much order value are we losing because customers cannot successfully pay?

A useful payment review should therefore examine performance by:

- country
- payment method
- provider
- transaction size
- device
- new versus repeat customer
- payment status
- retry outcome

Key metrics include:

- payment completion rate
- failed-payment rate
- checkout abandonment
- retry recovery rate
- pending transactions
- payment confirmation time
- payment-related support cases
- refund completion time

A lower processing fee is not automatically better if the payment route causes more customers to abandon checkout.

The better commercial question is: How much profitable order value does this payment setup help us complete, and what does it cost us to move and manage that money?

## What Is the True Cost of a Cross-Border E-commerce Payment?

The true cost of a cross-border payment is the difference between what the customer pays and what the business ultimately retains and can use, after processing fees, FX costs, payouts, refunds, disputes, settlement-related deductions and relevant operational costs.

The processing fee is only one part of that equation.

## Processing fees

A provider may charge a percentage, a fixed amount or both.

A small difference on one transaction can become significant when applied across thousands of payments.

## Currency conversion costs

If customers pay in one currency while your business settles in another, currency conversion can affect the final amount you receive.

Establish:

- exchange rate applied
- FX spread
- conversion charge
- settlement currency
- final amount received

## Payout costs

Moving funds from the payment environment to your preferred account can introduce additional charges depending on the payment and settlement arrangement.

## Refunds and disputes

Refunds, chargebacks and reversals can create costs beyond the original transaction.

Confirm:

- whether processing fees are returned
- whether FX costs are recoverable
- what happens to payout charges
- what dispute or chargeback fees apply
- how deductions appear in settlement reports

## Settlement and cash availability

A payment can be successfully collected but still unavailable to the business.

Review:

- settlement timing
- settlement currency
- settlement destination
- reserves or holds
- weekend and holiday treatment
- reversal and dispute deductions

## Operational costs

Your finance and operations teams may also spend time reconciling:

- provider reports
- orders
- settlement batches
- different currencies
- refunds
- failed transactions
- disputes
- payment discrepancies

A payment route can therefore look inexpensive at checkout while creating substantial costs elsewhere.

## How Much Are Failed Payments Costing Your Business?

Failed payments represent potential order value that did not become a completed sale, although some of that value may be recovered through retries or alternative payment methods.

For example, suppose your business receives:

- 10,000 payment attempts
- 800 failed attempts
- $75 average order value

The failed attempts represent:

**800 × $75 = $60,000 of attempted order value.**

That does not mean the business definitely lost $60,000.

Some customers may:

- retry successfully
- use another payment method
- return later
- complete the purchase through another route

The more useful question is:

How much of that failed order value could realistically have been recovered?

Track:

- failed-payment value
- successful retries
- abandoned payment attempts
- failure rate by market
- failure rate by payment method
- customers who complete later
- pending payment volume
- payment-related support cases

This allows you to distinguish between temporary payment friction and the demand you really lost.

## Where African E-commerce Payments Lose Money or Delay Settlement

A customer's payment and your final settlement are different stages of the payment journey.

Understanding each stage helps identify where value is being lost or delayed.

## 1. Customer payment

The customer selects an available payment method and attempts to pay.

Depending on the market, this could include:

- cards
- bank transfers
- mobile money
- local wallets
- other locally relevant account-based methods

The first question is whether customers actually have access to the methods they prefer to use.

## 2. Payment processing

The payment moves through the relevant payment infrastructure.

Evaluate:

- payment availability
- reliability
- transaction status
- failure rates
- applicable costs

## 3. Currency conversion

If the customer's payment currency differs from your settlement currency, conversion may affect the final amount.

Review:

- exchange rate
- FX spread
- conversion charge
- final settlement amount

## 4. Settlement

The processed funds become available according to the agreed settlement arrangement.

Confirm:

- when funds become available
- settlement currency
- settlement destination
- reserves or holds
- weekend and holiday treatment
- reversal and dispute treatment

## 5. Reconciliation

Payment activity must be matched against orders, refunds and accounting records.

This is where businesses can discover that a successful customer payment is not necessarily the same thing as immediately usable merchant funds.

The business may still be waiting for settlement, resolving a discrepancy, converting currency or matching a settlement batch to individual orders.

## How to Evaluate Payment Coverage Across African Markets

African markets do not share one identical payment environment. Merchants should evaluate payment methods, currencies, eligibility, settlement and operational requirements separately for every market they serve.

For each priority market, establish:

| Question | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Customer market | Is the country supported for your business model? |
| Payment method | Which methods are customers most likely to use? |
| Currency | What will customers pay in and what will you receive? |
| Merchant eligibility | Can your business onboard from its current entity and structure? |
| Settlement | Where will funds settle, when will they arrive and in what currency? |
| Limits | Are transaction and volume limits suitable? |
| Refunds | Can refunds be processed appropriately? |
| Compliance | What KYC, KYB, AML and licensing requirements apply? |
| Reliability | How are failures and incidents handled? |
| Reporting | Can activity be tracked by country, currency and payment method? |

One distinction is particularly important:

Customer-country support and merchant-country eligibility are not the same thing.

A provider may support customers in a particular country without providing the merchant onboarding, settlement structure or payment methods your business requires.

## How to Choose a Payment Gateway for Cross-Border E-commerce in Africa

There is no single payment infrastructure model that is automatically best for every merchant.

The appropriate model depends on:

- markets served
- transaction volume
- customer payment methods
- currencies
- settlement requirements
- technical environment
- operational complexity
- resilience requirements

### One payment provider

**Best suited to:** merchants seeking a simpler provider relationship.

**Advantage:** fewer integrations and commercial relationships.

**Trade-off:** coverage, payment-method availability, eligibility and settlement need to be strong enough across all priority markets.

### Multiple local providers

**Best suited to:** merchants prioritising specific countries.

**Advantage:** strong local payment coverage.

**Trade-off:** additional integrations, contracts, settlement relationships and support channels.

### Regional or pan-African payment infrastructure

**Best suited to:** merchants expanding across multiple African markets.

**Advantage:** potentially broader coverage through fewer provider relationships.

**Trade-off:** actual country coverage, payment methods, eligibility and settlement must be verified.

### International payment providers

**Best suited to:** merchants prioritising international card acceptance or global payment infrastructure.

**Advantage:** established technology and international infrastructure.

**Trade-off:** local payment methods and market-specific settlement may be more limited.

### Payment orchestration or multi-provider infrastructure

**Best suited to:** larger merchants requiring routing flexibility or redundancy.

**Advantage:** greater control over routing and payment resilience.

**Trade-off:** greater technical, reporting and operational complexity.

The objective isn't simply to minimise the number of providers but to build the simplest payment setup that provides the coverage, reliability, economics and resilience your business actually needs.

## What Should a Better African E-commerce Payment Setup Look Like?

A better payment setup should connect the entire payment operation rather than treating checkout as an isolated function.

At minimum, evaluate:

### Collection

Can customers use relevant payment methods in the markets you serve?

### Payout

Can required outbound payment flows be supported?

### Settlement

Can collected funds reach the appropriate account, currency, and destination under predictable terms?

### Integration

Can payment infrastructure connect to your existing e-commerce and finance environment?

### Reporting

Can your teams monitor payment performance, settlement and reconciliation?

Can your business maintain suitable payment coverage if a particular route experiences problems?

The goal is to create a payment operation that supports conversion, collection, settlement, reconciliation and growth.

## How to Test a New Payment Setup Before Expanding

You don't necessarily need to replace your entire payment infrastructure at once.

A controlled rollout can help you establish whether a new configuration actually improves commercial and operational performance.

## 1. Start with your priority markets

Document:

- current markets
- planned markets
- expected transaction volume
- average order value
- customer payment methods
- payment currencies
- settlement currencies
- settlement destinations

## 2. Map your payment flows

Document what happens after a customer makes a payment, from processing and conversion through to settlement and reconciliation.

The same exercise should be performed for refunds and relevant outbound payment flows.

## 3. Establish your baseline

Measure:

- payment completion
- failed payments
- processing cost
- FX cost
- payout cost
- refunds and disputes
- settlement time
- reconciliation workload
- net amount received

## 4. Decide what should change

Possible actions include:

- optimise the current provider
- add a payment method
- add another provider
- change payment routing
- review settlement arrangements
- improve reconciliation
- test a new infrastructure provider

## 5. Confirm onboarding and compliance

Establish requirements for:

- merchant eligibility
- KYC/KYB
- AML
- licensing
- transaction limits
- reserves
- settlement
- commercial terms

## 6. Integrate and test

Test:

- successful payments
- failed payments
- pending payments
- refunds
- disputes
- settlement
- transaction statuses
- reporting
- reconciliation

## 7. Launch and measure

Compare before and after:

- payment completion
- failure rate
- retry recovery
- final settlement cost
- settlement timing
- refund handling
- support workload
- reconciliation workload
- net amount received

Use those results to determine whether the configuration should expand to additional markets.

## Frequently Asked Questions

## What is the biggest payment cost for cross-border e-commerce merchants in Africa?

The highest cost depends on the merchant's payment mix, but it can come from processing fees, FX conversion, failed transactions, payouts, disputes, settlement deductions or operational work. Merchants should calculate total payment cost rather than relying on the headline processing rate.

## How do payment failures affect e-commerce revenue?

Payment failures can reduce revenue by preventing customers from completing otherwise viable purchases. The commercial impact depends on failure volume, average order value, retry recovery, and whether customers abandon checkout completely.

## How should merchants compare African payment gateways?

Compare payment completion, [local payment-method coverage](/resources/choose-payment-gateway), total cost, FX, settlement, payouts, refunds, reconciliation, integration, support and expansion capability, not processing fees alone.

## What payment methods should e-commerce businesses support across African markets?

The appropriate methods depend on the market and customer base. Cards, bank transfers, mobile money and other local account-based methods may be relevant, but merchants should evaluate actual customer behaviour and provider availability in each target market.

## Does supporting a customer country mean a merchant can operate there?

No. Customer-country payment support and merchant eligibility are separate questions. A provider may support customers in a country without providing the onboarding, settlement or business structure your merchant operation requires.

## How important is settlement when choosing a payment provider?

Settlement is critical because successfully collected funds are not necessarily immediately usable funds. Merchants should confirm settlement timing, currency, destination, reserves, holds and how disputes or reversals affect settlement.

## Should an e-commerce merchant use one payment provider or multiple providers?

It depends on the merchant's markets, payment-method coverage, transaction volume and resilience requirements. One provider can simplify operations, while multiple providers may provide stronger local coverage or redundancy. The goal is to find the simplest setup that provides the coverage and reliability the business needs.
