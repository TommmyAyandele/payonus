## The gap between approval and disbursement is where lenders lose borrowers

Most lending platforms have solved the hard part — underwriting a loan decision in seconds using data most lenders didn't have access to a decade ago. What often hasn't kept pace is what happens after approval: if disbursement takes days instead of minutes, the speed of the underwriting decision stops mattering to the borrower, who experiences the whole process as slow regardless of how fast the algorithm was.

Instant disbursement — triggering a payout the moment a loan is approved, straight to a bank account or mobile wallet — is what actually closes that gap between decision and money in hand.

## Repayment collection needs to be as automatic as disbursement

The inverse problem shows up on the repayment side. Manually chasing repayments — sending reminders, processing individual transfers — doesn't scale past a small loan book. Automated repayment collection, scheduled or triggered on demand across card, bank transfer, USSD, or mobile money, is what lets a lending operation grow its loan book without growing its collections team at the same rate.

## Remittance is a trust business built on payout reliability

For a remittance platform, the product is really a promise: send money here, and it arrives there, reliably, at the rate quoted. Every failed or delayed payout erodes that promise for both the sender and the recipient — and unlike a failed e-commerce transaction, a failed remittance often means money someone was depending on for something specific, arriving late.

That's why remittance corridors live or die on payout rail reliability more than almost any other payment use case. Settlement directly to recipient bank accounts and mobile wallets, across every market a corridor serves, is the baseline requirement, not a differentiator.

## PSSPs and aggregators need infrastructure to build on, not compete with

Payment service providers and aggregators occupy an unusual position: they're not the end merchant, but they're also not the underlying rail. What they need from infrastructure is different from either — sub-account structures so their own users can hold and move funds, ledger infrastructure that doesn't require building a mini-bank internally, and reconciliation that works across every downstream account, not just at the top level.

A PSSP trying to build all of this from scratch is effectively trying to become the infrastructure company it should be renting instead.

## What non-bank fintechs should look for

Whether the business is a lender, a remittance platform, or a PSSP, the underlying question is the same: does the payment infrastructure move money as fast as the product's core logic runs, or does it become the bottleneck. That's the right lens for evaluating any payment provider in this space — not just whether it processes payments, but whether it can move at the speed the fintech's own product already promises.
