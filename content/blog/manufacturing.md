## The transfer fee is not the full cost of paying a supplier

The invoice says one amount. Finance approves that amount. The payment goes out. Then the supplier reports receiving less.

The stated transfer fee is usually the easiest cost to see, and the least useful one for understanding what a payment actually costs. The real cost can include the FX spread, correspondent or intermediary-bank deductions, receiving-bank charges, conversion costs, and whatever it takes internally to figure out why the numbers don't match. None of those show up on the fee schedule a provider advertises.

A provider charging a small transfer fee can still produce an expensive payment if the exchange rate carries a wide spread, or if a bank somewhere in the routing chain deducts a charge before the funds land. When that happens, the manufacturer often has to send a top-up just to make the supplier whole — which means another transaction, more FX exposure, more finance work, and a possible delay if the supplier won't release goods until the shortfall is covered.

The question worth asking isn't which provider charges the least to send money. It's what the payment will actually cost from end to end, and how much the supplier will actually receive.

## Why suppliers sometimes receive less than what was sent

A $100,000 invoice can arrive as $98,700 for several unrelated reasons: the exchange rate applied, a deduction taken by an intermediary bank, a charge applied by the receiving bank, or some combination of the three. Figuring out which one happened after the fact is exactly the kind of reconciliation work that eats finance-team hours.

If the supplier won't release materials or equipment until the full amount clears, a $1,300 shortfall stops being an accounting footnote and starts being a shipment delay. A payment setup worth using should show, before the money moves, what's being sent, what rate applies, what fees are being charged, and what the supplier should expect to receive — not just confirmation that a transaction was initiated.

## Every payment should be traceable from invoice to settlement

A controlled supplier-payment process connects the purchase order and invoice to the beneficiary, the payment itself, the supplier's confirmation of receipt, and the accounting entry that closes it out. At any point, finance should be able to answer which invoice was paid, what currency and rate were used, what the payment cost, whether any deductions were applied, and whether the transaction has been reconciled.

When that chain isn't connected, reconstructing a transaction means pulling together payment confirmations, bank statements, supplier emails, and spreadsheets by hand — and doing it under time pressure, usually because something already went wrong.

## A payment delay is rarely just a finance problem

Manufacturers depend on suppliers for raw materials, components, machinery, spare parts, and packaging. If a supplier hasn't received cleared funds, none of that gets released on schedule, which can cascade into freight delays, customs holdups, and missed customer commitments. The goal isn't to move money faster for its own sake — it's to remove the uncertainty about when a supplier will actually have the funds needed to fulfil an order.

## Comparing payment models

There isn't one cross-border payment setup that fits every manufacturer; the right one depends on supplier locations, currencies, volumes, and how much visibility finance needs.

Traditional bank or SWIFT transfers work for established relationships and larger, less frequent transactions, but processing time, intermediary routing, and total deductions are worth checking corridor by corridor. Local bank accounts can make sense where a manufacturer has real presence in a market, at the cost of managing multiple onboarding processes and separate reconciliation. Dedicated cross-border payment platforms can simplify execution across supported countries, provided the coverage, limits, and settlement destinations actually match the manufacturer's needs. FX or treasury providers suit businesses with heavier currency-management requirements, but the full workflow — execution, supplier receipt, settlement, and reconciliation — needs evaluating, not just the FX pricing. Running several providers across different corridors is sometimes unavoidable, but it multiplies contracts, reports, and reconciliation work. More providers isn't the goal; the right combination of cost control, certainty, and visibility is.

## What to check before committing to a provider

Before choosing, it's worth confirming corridor and currency coverage for the specific countries suppliers are in, the all-in cost of a payment (rate, spread, fees, and any deductions along the route), how quickly and reliably funds actually settle, whether finance can link payments to purchase orders and invoices for reconciliation, and whether the provider's API, dashboards, and reporting can connect to the systems already in use rather than becoming another disconnected workflow.

## Rolling out a new setup without disrupting existing operations

A practical way to test a new supplier-payment setup is to start with the corridors causing the most cost or delay, document the current approval and reconciliation process, confirm the currencies and settlement arrangements actually needed, and test the flow — including delayed, returned, and exception cases — before expanding to additional suppliers or markets. That sequencing lets a manufacturer validate the payment operation before it becomes load-bearing for the rest of the business.

Manufacturers generally don't need to replace their ERP, procurement, or treasury systems to get better visibility. API-based payment infrastructure can connect to what's already there, which is the approach Payonus takes for the businesses it works with — building the payment layer to fit existing finance and procurement workflows rather than requiring a manufacturer to rebuild around it.

The underlying goal is straightforward: knowing, for every supplier payment, what it cost, when it settled, and whether the supplier actually received what was expected. That's what protects supplier relationships, production schedules, and margin — not the number printed on the fee schedule.
